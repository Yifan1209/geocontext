Visual geolocation benchmarks ask a model where a photograph was taken and score how far off it is. Real users rarely ask that in isolation: they volunteer where they think they are -- "I took this near X" -- and that sentence is usually true but imprecise. How a model should weigh such a hint is unmeasured, and the same weighing governs a deployed decision: whether a photograph confirms a location claim, as when a platform asks a driver to photograph a drop-off.

We present GeoContext, one resource supporting two tasks: GeoHint, open-ended localization given a true but coarse hint, and GeoVerify, binary verification that an image was taken within 150 m of a claimed place, scored with signal detection theory. The shared resource is a context ladder that stratifies mined reference points by distance (0.5-6 km) and by referenceability -- how often people use a place to say where they are, which we show is distinct from fame -- so that the image is held fixed while only the hint varies. The release covers 109 sites in 30 cities, 5 VLMs, and 21,933 scored GeoHint and 6270 GeoVerify responses.

Our evaluation yields three main findings. Reliance on the hint is flat along both axes -- 1.5 points across referenceability, under 3 across distance -- while the error that relying produces grows steadily, so the cost scales and the reliance does not. Where the imagery is illegible the median ratio of error to hint distance is 1.00: the answer is the hint. Where it is legible that ratio falls to 0.24 to 0.69, and one model of five is measurably less accurate with a hint than without one. On GeoVerify no model reaches d' = 1 at the tolerance a deployment needs, ranking by accuracy inverts the ordering because models differ sharply in response bias, and 83.8% of false acceptances are stated at confidence >= 0.8. Code, data and every audit decision are released.
